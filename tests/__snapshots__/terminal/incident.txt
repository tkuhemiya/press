
  Anatomy of an outage
  ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

  Most outages are short. What makes them expensive is the gap between something
  breaking and someone noticing, and the gap between noticing and acting. This
  page lays out one afternoon’s incident on a clock, so those gaps are visible.

  ## The afternoon of 14 March

  Outage, 14 March

     14:02  ●  v2.3 rolls out ① [a]
            │
  ╭─ Impact window
  │  14:09  ●  ✗ Error rate hits 8% [b]
  │         │  ↖ from “v2.3 rolls out”: caused ②
  │         │
  │  14:12  ●┓ Deploys frozen [d]
  │         │┃
  │  14:15  ●┃ Rollback starts ③ [c]
  │         │┃
  │  14:21  ●┃ ✓ Errors back to baseline ④
  ╰─        │┃
     14:40  │┛ └ end of “Deploys frozen”
            │
     14:40  ●  Freeze lifted

  ① A routine deploy goes out. Nothing looks wrong yet.
  ② Seven minutes later the error rate crosses the alert threshold.
  ③ The on-call engineer rolls back instead of debugging live.
  ④ Six minutes after that, errors are back to normal.

  The seven minutes between the deploy[a] and the alert[b] are the detection
  gap; the alert threshold sets its length. The rollback[c] started three
  minutes after the alert, which is fast because rolling back needed no
  diagnosis. The freeze[d] outlasted the impact on purpose: nobody deploys again
  until the cause is understood.

  ## How the fix rolled out

  Undated timelines keep only the order, which is enough for a sequence of
  stages.

  The fix

  ●  Reproduce the bug in staging
  │
  ●  Add a failing test
  │  ↖ from “Reproduce the bug in staging”
  │
  ●  Patch the retry logic
  │  one line
  │
  ◆  Ship to 5% of traffic [a]
  │
  ●  ✓ Ship to everyone

  The canary[a] is the step that would have caught the original bug.
